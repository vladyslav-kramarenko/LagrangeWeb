Learning guide

How Lagrange polynomial interpolation works

Learn how to build a polynomial from known points, understand the formula, and try a complete example.

On this page The method The formula Worked example Use the calculator FAQ
01 / OVERVIEW

What is Lagrange interpolation?

Lagrange polynomial interpolation finds a polynomial that passes exactly through the data points you supply. If there are n + 1 points with distinct X coordinates, there is exactly one interpolating polynomial of degree at most n.

Unlike polynomial regression, interpolation matches the input values at every supplied X coordinate. That makes it useful when you want to reconstruct a curve from known measurements or understand interpolation in numerical methods.

02 / FORMULA

The Lagrange interpolation formula

For points (x₀, y₀), (x₁, y₁), …, (xₙ, yₙ), write the polynomial as a weighted sum of basis polynomials:

INTERPOLATING POLYNOMIAL
P(x) = Σ yᵢ · Lᵢ(x)
Sum over i = 0, 1, …, n

Each basis polynomial Lᵢ(x) equals 1 at its own X coordinate and 0 at the other supplied X coordinates:

LAGRANGE BASIS
Lᵢ(x) = ∏ (x − xⱼ) / (xᵢ − xⱼ)
Multiply over all j from 0 to n except j = i

Multiplying each basis polynomial by its Y value and adding the terms creates the final interpolation polynomial.

03 / WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: interpolate three points

Suppose you want a polynomial through these coordinates:

PointXY
112
225
3310

Step 1. Build the basis polynomials

For each point, use the other two X coordinates:

L₀(x) = (x − 2)(x − 3) / 2
L₁(x) = −(x − 1)(x − 3)
L₂(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2) / 2

Step 2. Weight by Y and add

Use the three Y values, 2, 5, and 10:

SUM
P(x) = 2L₀(x) + 5L₁(x) + 10L₂(x)

Step 3. Simplify and check

Expanding and collecting like terms gives:

RESULTP(x) = x² + 1

Check the inputs: P(1) = 2, P(2) = 5, and P(3) = 10. The polynomial passes through all three points.

05 / FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How many points do I need?

Two distinct X coordinates define a line. Three points define a polynomial of degree at most two, four points of degree at most three, and so on.

Can two points have the same X value?

Not for standard Lagrange interpolation. Two identical X coordinates make the basis formula divide by zero, even if the Y values are equal.

Is interpolation the same as regression?

No. Interpolation passes through all supplied points; regression usually seeks an approximate fit and can be better for noisy measurements.

Can I use negative or decimal values?

Yes. The X and Y coordinates can be negative or decimal numbers. For very many points or extreme values, floating-point rounding may affect numerical accuracy.
